Episode 8. Scenario-based questions, and the resume grill


Scenario questions sound like this: "It's Monday morning, the executive dashboard is wrong, what do you do?" They test judgement under pressure, more than knowledge. The structure that works every time is: stabilise, diagnose, fix, prevent, and communicate. Stabilise means stop the damage first. Diagnose means find the real cause, not the first symptom. Fix means the smallest safe change. Prevent means the change to the system so it doesn't recur. And communicate means telling the right people early, with facts and a time for the next update.

There are ten scenarios in this episode, then the resume grill. For each one, pause, answer out loud, then compare with a model answer written the way you'd actually say it.

Scenario one: "The executive dashboard showed wrong numbers this morning, and the CFO noticed." Pause and answer.
(Pause: answer out loud.)
Here's how I'd say it. "First, I'd tell the CFO's office straight away that we know, and we're on it, with a time for the next update. If the wrong numbers are still showing, I'd restore yesterday's good version of the table with Delta time travel, so nobody makes decisions on bad data. Then I'd trace back with lineage in Unity Catalog, from the dashboard table to its sources, and use table history to see which job wrote what, and when. Usually it's a late or duplicated load, a schema change upstream, or a join that started multiplying rows. Once it's fixed and reprocessed, I'd add checks on those key metrics, reconciliation against the source, and a freshness alert, so next time we catch it before the CFO does."

Scenario two: "A critical nightly pipeline failed at 3 a.m. and the business needs data by 9." Pause and answer.
(Pause: answer out loud.)
"I'd read the run error and the driver logs first. If it's a transient failure, like a cloud hiccup, I'd use repair run, which reruns only the failed tasks and what depends on them, so we don't redo hours of work. If it's bad data, like a corrupt file or an unexpected schema, I'd quarantine the bad records and load everything else, so the business gets most of its data by nine, and I'd tell them exactly what's missing and when it'll arrive. Afterwards, I'd add retries with backoff on the flaky step, a rescued data column in bronze, and a runbook, so the next on-call engineer can fix it in minutes."

Scenario three: "A source team changed their schema without telling anyone, and downstream jobs broke." Pause and answer.
(Pause: answer out loud.)
"Short term, bronze should keep loading, because it evolves the schema or puts unexpected fields into a rescued data column. So I'd fix the silver mapping for the new columns and reprocess. Then the longer fix, which is as much about people as technology: a data contract with the source team, covering the schema, freshness and how changes are announced, a schema-drift alert, and a contract test in CI. I'd go and talk to that team, not just raise a ticket."

Scenario four: "The monthly Databricks bill jumped forty percent." Pause and answer.
(Pause: answer out loud.)
"I'd break the increase down from the billing system tables, by workspace, compute type, job and tag, and find the top few lines, because it's almost always a handful of things. Typically a production job running on an all-purpose cluster, clusters with no auto-termination, a stream running all day when it only needed to refresh every few minutes, or an oversized SQL warehouse. I'd fix the biggest ones first, then add guardrails: cluster policies, mandatory tags, budgets with alerts, and a monthly cost review where each owner sees their own spend."

Scenario five: "A query that used to take two minutes now takes forty." Pause and answer.
(Pause: answer out loud.)
"First question: what changed? Data volume, the query, the table, or the runtime. Then I'd open the query profile and look for the obvious culprits: it's suddenly reading far more files, it's spilling to disk, or one task is much slower than the rest. Common causes are small files from a new streaming writer, a table that lost its clustering, or a join that stopped being broadcast because one side grew. The fix is usually OPTIMIZE or turning on predictive optimization, clustering on the filter columns, or a broadcast hint. Then I'd add that query to a regression check."

Scenario six: "An analyst says they can see customer phone numbers they shouldn't." Pause and answer.
(Pause: answer out loud.)
"I'd treat it as a security incident. First, restrict access immediately, and keep the evidence. Then the audit logs in system tables tell me who saw what, and when, and how it happened: a missing mask, or a grant that was too broad. I'd fix the control, ideally with a tag-based policy, so every table tagged as containing phone numbers is masked automatically, not just this one. And I'd follow the privacy incident process: tell security and the privacy officer, assess whether it's notifiable, and record what we did. Longer term: tokenisation at ingestion and regular access reviews."

Scenario seven: "A Unity Catalog migration is three months late and the client is frustrated." Pause and answer.
(Pause: answer out loud.)
"First I'd find out why it's late. It's usually hidden complexity: data in the DBFS root, mount points, init scripts, or teams that haven't changed their code. Then I'd have an honest conversation with the sponsor: what's done, what's left, the real risks, and a new plan. And I'd change the approach: smaller waves by domain, the UCX tool to automate the inventory and table upgrades, a migration engineer working alongside each team, and visible progress every week. Trust comes back from delivered waves, not promises."

Scenario eight: "Two business units want conflicting definitions of 'active customer'." Pause and answer.
(Pause: answer out loud.)
"I wouldn't pick a winner myself. I'd get both owners in a room with the data, and show exactly where their definitions differ and what each one is used for. Very often both are right for different purposes, so we name them differently, like active billing customer and active network customer, and define each once, as a governed metric with an owner. If they still can't agree, it goes to the data governance forum."

Scenario nine: "The client's security team blocks your Databricks deployment until it's fully private." Pause and answer.
(Pause: answer out loud.)
"I'd treat security as a requirement, not an obstacle, and start by agreeing their exact requirements. Then I'd propose the standard pattern: VNet injection, secure cluster connectivity so clusters have no public IPs, egress through a NAT gateway or their firewall, front-end and back-end Private Link, private endpoints for storage and Key Vault with public access off, network connectivity configurations for serverless, and private DNS zones. I'd bring a diagram and a test plan, and bring their network team in early, because DNS is where it usually breaks."

Scenario ten: "Your project's budget gets cut by a third halfway through." Pause and answer.
(Pause: answer out loud.)
"I'd go back to the sponsor and ask which outcomes matter most, then re-plan to deliver those first. I'd cut scope, not quality: defer the lower-value features, simplify where we can, for example serverless instead of custom clusters, and reuse frameworks we already have. I'd put the trade-off in writing: what they'll get, what they won't, and the risks. Then deliver the smaller scope really well."

Now the resume grill. These are the questions an interviewer asks after reading your CV, to test that the claims are real. Short answers.

"How did you move data out of AWS and GCP, and what did egress cost?" Name the transfer method, how you batched the transfers to control egress, and how you verified completeness before each source was switched off. Use your real numbers.

"What does commercial launch mean for an internal platform?" The platform became the system of record for retrieval, and the legacy systems could be decommissioned. Use your real example, ideally with savings or the number of systems retired.

"How did you reconcile billions of migrated records?" Reconciliation was built into the framework: every load wrote source and target row counts and checksums on key columns, mismatches failed the load and alerted, and the biggest tables also had sampled row-level hash comparisons.

"How did the search app handle unstructured data?" Explain how files in Unity Catalog volumes were indexed and searched, and how document access was governed per user.

"When would you not use Databricks Apps?" Public-facing or very high-concurrency apps, sub-second latency at scale, or complex front ends better served by a dedicated web platform. Apps suit internal, governed, data-centric tools.

"Ninety-five percent on-time delivery: how is that measured, and which one did you miss?" Give the measure, then one real miss and what you changed afterwards.

"How do you lead a team split across two time zones?" Overlap hours for design decisions, written design documents so the offshore team isn't blocked waiting for onshore, clear ownership per workstream, rotating demo leads, and code reviews across both sites.

"You've only used Azure deeply. How would this change on AWS?" Same lakehouse and Unity Catalog design. Swap ADLS for S3, managed identities for IAM roles, VNet injection for a customer-managed VPC, and Private Link for AWS PrivateLink. If you've moved data from other clouds, mention it.

"How would you stand up a new client's platform?" Terraform with the Databricks provider for the account, metastore, workspaces, catalogs, grants and cluster policies. Bundles for jobs, pipelines and apps. CI/CD to promote between environments.

"Why didn't you use declarative pipelines?" The framework predates them and needed custom control-table logic. For new builds, I'd use them for standard medallion pipelines with expectations and AUTO CDC.
