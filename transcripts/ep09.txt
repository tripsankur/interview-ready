Episode 9. The managerial round: STAR stories, leadership and consulting


The managerial round tests judgement more than technology: ownership, stakeholders, leading people, handling clients. At a consultancy, picture the interviewer asking one question: "Can I put this person in front of a client on their own?"

Quick recap of STAR. Situation in about fifteen seconds. Task in about ten. Action in about sixty: what you did, in the first person. Result in about fifteen, with a number, plus one line on what you learned.

You need about ten stories, covering the themes panels ask about most. Here are seven story types with example shapes, using a fictional data platform lead. Replace every detail with your own real version and real numbers.

Story one, for "your biggest achievement" or "end-to-end ownership". Situation: the company was decommissioning legacy systems across several clouds, but had to keep their data for regulatory retention. Task: as design and delivery lead, take a new archival platform from design through security certification to launch. Action: I designed the lakehouse and the metadata-driven history-load framework. I led security certification, using Unity Catalog grants, row filters and column masks. And I managed the change board, vendor and security engagements to keep delivery compliant. Then the hardest decision you made and why. Result: the scale in records and tables, the load performance gain, and that it was certified and launched.

Story two, for "delivering under pressure", "ambiguity" or "customer focus". Situation: non-technical teams depended on engineers to retrieve archived data for regulatory requests. Task: give them governed self-service without weakening security. Action: I scoped a minimum version with the users raising the most requests. I architected a Databricks App with on-behalf-of authentication, so security applied per user. I released early and iterated. Result: weeks from idea to production, and the number of users.

Story three, for "stakeholder management", "risk" or "a difficult stakeholder". Situation: privacy law requires personal data isn't kept longer than needed, and retention wasn't enforced consistently. Task: lead an automated purging programme that legal, security and business owners would trust. Action: I worked with legal to turn policy into retention rules per data object, designed the purge so data is physically removed with audit evidence, and got business owners to agree retention periods. Result: records purged, objects covered, and how widely it's now used.

Story four, for "influence without authority". Situation: many teams wanted to build apps on the platform, but there was no standard and a real risk of insecure apps. Task: let them build safely without my team becoming a bottleneck. Action: I wrote the standard and built a deployment pipeline that bakes it in, so following the standard is the easy path. Then how you won buy-in. Result: how many builders release through it, and any external recognition like a conference talk.

Story five, for "a technical trade-off under risk". Analysts needed to join on customer identifiers, but those identifiers were a privacy risk. I chose deterministic, keyed tokenisation, so joins still work, with keys in a secrets vault, built into ingestion so every load is protected by default. Result: the scale protected.

Story six, for "leading and mentoring". I led a small team of engineers on a critical platform. I introduced code reviews and shared standards, mentored juniors, and tuned the slowest jobs. Result: delivery and performance improvements, as percentages.

Story seven, for "business impact". A process change or automation you designed, and the revenue gained or cost saved, in dollars.

Story eight: a disagreement, and story nine: a failure. These must be real, so prepare them yourself. For disagreement, use this shape: what you disagreed about and why it mattered, how you listened first, the data or options you brought, how it was resolved, whether you changed your mind, they did, or you disagreed and committed, and how the relationship is now. For failure: own it in the first sentence, explain how you recovered, and what you changed so it doesn't happen again. Never blame the team or another department.

Quick mapping. Biggest achievement: story one. Conflict: your disagreement story, with story three as backup. Influence: story four. Tight deadline or ambiguity: story two. Leading people: story six. Business impact: story seven. Technical trade-off: story five. Have a backup for each, so you don't reuse the same story three times in one interview.

Now leadership questions.

"What's your leadership style?" "Hands-on but not controlling. I set the direction and the standards, and I stay close to design. I run the design reviews and I can still read and write the code. Then I give engineers clear ownership of workstreams and hold them to outcomes. With a team split across time zones, that also means writing things down, so nobody waits on a meeting to make progress."

"How do you handle an underperformer?" Here's how I'd say it. "I raise it early and privately, with specific examples, not general impressions. Then I try to find the cause, because skill, motivation and something going on outside work each need a different fix. We agree clear expectations and the support I'll give, like pairing or a smaller scope for a while, with dates. I check in every week or two. Most people turn it around. If someone doesn't after real support, I bring in HR, but by then it's never a surprise to them." Then add a short, anonymised real example.

"A project is slipping. What do you do?" "I try to find out early, from signs like blocked work or a slipping dependency, not on the due date. I find the real cause before fixing anything. Then I re-plan, and if something has to give, I cut scope before quality. I tell stakeholders straight away, with options and my recommendation, for example: we can deliver the core on the original date and the rest two weeks later. And afterwards I fix whatever caused it."

Consulting questions. "What do you do in your first two weeks with a client?" Here's how I'd say it. "Listen, map, agree, and earn trust. In the first week I interview the sponsor, the data owners, the platform team, security and a few end users, to learn what they actually need and what's painful today. I map the current architecture and data flows. Then I agree success measures and constraints with the sponsor, in writing. I look for one quick win I can deliver in the first fortnight, like fixing a slow pipeline or a cost leak, to build trust. And I finish with a short written readout: what I found, the options, and what I recommend next."

"How do you estimate a Databricks engagement?" "I break it into workstreams: platform setup, ingestion, governance, consumption and migration. Then I size each one by things I can count, like the number of sources, pipelines and tables, each rated simple, medium or complex with a standard effort. I add discovery, testing, cutover and hypercare, then an explicit contingency. And I write down every assumption, because those are what you negotiate later. A metadata-driven framework helps a lot: once it exists, each new source has a known, small cost."

"The client insists on a technical decision you think is wrong." "First I find out why they want it, because there's often a constraint I can't see, like a team skill, a contract or a security rule. Then I explain the risks in business terms, offer alternatives and recommend one. If they still choose their way, and it isn't unsafe or non-compliant, I write down the decision and the risk, and then I commit fully to making it work. It's their platform. I only escalate for security, compliance or serious delivery risk."

A few more you should have ready, briefly.

"Tell me about giving difficult feedback." "I give it soon after it happens, in private, and I'm specific: what happened and the impact it had, not what kind of person they are. Then I listen, because there's often context I don't have. We agree what changes, and I follow up." Then give a real example, for example an engineer whose code reviews were harsh and slowing the team down.

"How do you keep up with Databricks?" "I read the release notes and the blog, go to Data and AI Summit and World Tour sessions, and try new features hands-on in a sandbox before I recommend them. The teams using our platform are a good signal too, because they tell me what's actually useful." Then name one recent feature you tried. A good one this year: Databricks renamed several products, so using the current names, like Lakeflow pipelines, Declarative Automation Bundles and Genie Agents, shows you're current.

"Where do you see yourself in three years?" "Leading Databricks engagements end to end for several clients, owning the relationship as well as the architecture, and helping build the practice: mentoring architects and building reusable accelerators like ingestion frameworks and app standards."

"Salary expectations and notice period?" Ask for their band first if you can: "What's the budgeted range for this role?" If pressed: "I'm looking for a total package, including super, in the range of" and give your range, "depending on the role and the overall offer. I'm flexible for the right opportunity. My notice period is" and give it. Always a range for the total package, never a single base number.

"How do you handle scope creep?" "I welcome the request, because it usually means they value the work, and then I run it through change control. I show the impact on time, cost and risk, and give options: swap it for something lower value, extend the timeline or budget, or put it in a later phase. The sponsor decides, and we record it. That way scope only changes on purpose."

"How do you present an architecture to a CIO or CFO?" "I lead with the business outcome and the cost, not the technology. For example: this cuts monthly reporting from five days to one, and costs this much a month to run. Then one simple diagram, two or three options with a clear recommendation, the risks, and what I need from them, usually a decision. The detailed architecture goes in an appendix for their architects."

"How do you hand over to the client's team?" "Handover starts on day one, not at the end. Their engineers pair with mine during the build, so they learn by doing. We write runbooks, architecture decision records and short training sessions as we go. We agree a support model and a few weeks of hypercare, and we define what handed over means up front: for example, their team has deployed a change and handled an incident without us."

Now the gap questions, because a good panel will find them. No Databricks certification: "Not yet. My experience is production at real scale," then your scale, then "I'm sitting the Data Engineer Professional exam on" and give the date. In-house rather than consulting: "I've run security, change board and vendor engagements, scoped work for internal customers, and presented externally. Internal stakeholders are clients without invoices."

And finally, questions to ask them. Pick two or three. "What does the first client engagement look like for this role, and is it already sold?" "How is the role split between pre-sales and delivery?" "Which Databricks capabilities are clients asking for most right now?" "How do you measure success in the first six months?"
