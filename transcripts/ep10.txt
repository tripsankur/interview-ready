Episode 10. The Senior Forward Deployed Engineer role


The Senior Forward Deployed Engineer role at Databricks used to be called Resident Solution Architect. It's a customer-embedded engineer: you sit with a strategic customer and build, deploy and debug production systems end to end, often Databricks Apps. It's more hands-on than an architect role, and the bar is evidence: production outcomes, role clarity, and certification.

What the profile asks for. Seven or more years in data engineering and platforms. Ten or more years of consulting. The Data Engineer Professional certification and required classes. At least six to eight Databricks projects delivered hands-on. Working knowledge of two or more clouds, deep in one. Deep Spark experience, including runtime internals. CI/CD for production. Current knowledge across the breadth of the platform. And performance and scalability optimisation. The core responsibilities: design and build Databricks Apps, front end and back end; deploy and monitor in production; and debug complex issues across the stack. What distinguishes a Forward Deployed Engineer: turning ambiguous requirements into production systems, full-stack delivery from ingestion to the application layer, and working autonomously in high-visibility engagements.

Assess your fit honestly, requirement by requirement, before the interview. Count your consulting years explicitly, including years at service companies delivering for clients, and frame in-house work as serving internal customers. If you're not certified yet, that's usually the biggest gap at senior level, because the profile lists the certification as completed. Book the exam now, and ask the recruiter whether a date before your start is acceptable. And find your standout strength against their list. If you've built production Databricks Apps, lead with it, because it's their first listed responsibility.

Remember the levelling point: Databricks calibrates candidates from Senior down to Associate based on demonstrated capability, not tenure. Decide before the interview whether you'd accept a non-senior offer.

Your project list. They want six to eight Databricks projects, each with your role and one outcome. Write them down before the interview in one line each: the project, what you personally designed, built or led, and a number. For example: an archival lakehouse, architect and delivery lead, with its record count. A metadata-driven load framework, designed and built, with the volume it has loaded. A self-service Databricks App, weeks to production and users. A governance standard and deployment pipeline, and how many builders use it. A tokenisation or privacy product, and the scale it covers. And be careful: a Spark platform on Hadoop is Spark depth, not a Databricks project, so don't count it.

Now the likely questions.

"Why Forward Deployed Engineer, and why Databricks?" Here's how I'd say it. "I'm at my best embedded with a team, turning a fuzzy problem into something running in production. That's what I've done in my current role: a self-service app that went from an idea to production in weeks. This role lets me do that with Databricks' most ambitious customers, with direct access to the product teams. And I've been building on Databricks Apps since early on, so I want to be closer to where it's shaped." Swap in your own example and timeline.

"Design a Databricks App for a customer, end to end." Here's how I'd say it. "First I'd ask who uses it, what decision it supports, how sensitive the data is, and how many people use it at once. Say it's an internal tool for a claims team with a few hundred users. I'd build the front end in React with a FastAPI back end, or Streamlit if speed matters more than polish. I'd use user authorization, so the app queries with each person's own token and Unity Catalog applies their grants, row filters and masks automatically. The app's own service principal only does shared, non-sensitive work. Analytical queries go to a serverless SQL warehouse. App state, like saved searches or approvals, goes in Lakebase, the managed Postgres, because it handles small fast writes far better than Delta. Anything slow, like a large export, triggers a job and polls, so a web request never blocks. Resources and secrets are declared in app dot yaml and the bundle, not hard-coded. And it ships through CI/CD to dev, test and prod, with logs and alerts. The app stays a thin layer over governed data."

"Walk me through debugging a complex issue across the stack." "I start from the symptom and work down through the layers, changing one thing at a time. Say users report the app is slow. App logs first: is it the app or the query? Then identity: is the query running as the user, with the right permissions? Then the query profile: is it scanning too many files, spilling or skewed? Then compute: is the warehouse queuing or scaling? Then the data: small files, lost clustering, a schema change in the table history? And if requests fail outright on a private deployment, the network: DNS, private endpoints and firewall rules." Then tell one real story, with the cause, the fix and the improvement.

"How do you deploy and monitor in production?" "Everything deploys from Git through CI/CD. Bundles define the jobs, pipelines and apps, and the same bundle goes from dev to test to prod, deployed by a service principal, never a personal token. For monitoring I watch three layers. Pipelines: job failure alerts, freshness checks, and data quality expectations that drop or fail bad rows, with a quarantine table for the ones we need to review. Data: reconciliation counts, and data quality monitoring for drift. Apps and models: app logs, health checks, and MLflow traces for anything with an LLM. Cost comes from system tables, in a dashboard owners actually look at. And every alert has a runbook, so the customer's team can handle it after I leave."

"The customer wants something Databricks can't do well." "I'd say so early, because trust is worth more than one workload. Say a customer wants thousands of small transactional writes a second, with millisecond reads, on Delta tables. I'd tell them plainly: Delta is built for analytics, and it isn't the right store for that pattern. Then I'd give them the best design. Often that's still Databricks, using Lakebase, the managed Postgres, for the transactional part, synced to the lakehouse for analytics. If it really needs another tool, I'd recommend it and show how it fits with their governed data. Then I'd write up the gap for the Databricks product team, with the customer's numbers. Being the customer's honest voice back into Databricks is part of the job."

"How do you work autonomously in a high-visibility engagement?" "In week one I agree success criteria with the sponsor, in writing: what's in scope, what done looks like, and the dates that matter. After that I show working software every week instead of status slides. I make reversible decisions myself, quickly. For anything hard to undo, like a networking design or a data model other teams depend on, I bring the sponsor a recommendation and the trade-off, and get a decision. Risks go up early, with options, never as a surprise. And I plan the handover from the start, so they can run it without me."

Breadth questions to revise, because the role expects current knowledge of the platform: Lakebase, Agent Bricks, Genie, Lakeflow Connect, Lakeflow pipelines, Model Serving and Unity Gateway, AI Search, serverless compute, predictive optimization, liquid clustering, Unity Catalog attribute-based access control and metric views, and OpenSharing. Also know the new names: Declarative Automation Bundles, Lakeflow pipelines, OpenSharing, AI Search, Unity Gateway and Genie Agents. For each, know what it is in one sentence and one situation where you'd use it.

And the Spark internals refresher: Catalyst, whole-stage code generation and Photon, stages at shuffle boundaries, adaptive query execution, unified memory and spill, and the three join strategies: broadcast hash, shuffle hash and sort-merge.

Finally, your profile, per Databricks' own guide. They favour measurable outcomes, clarity about what you led versus what the team did, certifications with dates, a GitHub link, and no more than two pages. And they explicitly penalise generic, AI-sounding wording. So in the interview, use plain verbs precisely. Say "I designed", "I built", "I led", and mean each one, because they'll test it.
